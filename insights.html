YouthScape: A Community Solution to Youth Bail and Accommodation in the Illawarra

Young children, some as young as 10, are coming before the Illawarra courts. They come from homes where parents are on drugs, where there is partying and chaos and violence, where homework cannot be done and sleep cannot be found. They disengage from school. They roam the streets at night. They get into stealing. Eventually they are caught. And at the point they are caught, the question of bail arises.

If the court decides that sending them back to the home they came from is not safe — and for many of these young people, it is not — then they are refused bail. Not because of what they have done. Not because they are a flight risk. But because there is nowhere safe for them to go. The only option is remand to a youth detention centre 70 kilometres from their community, separated from family, from school, from everything familiar.

YouthScape is IRAAC's proposed answer to this problem. A physical house in the Illawarra region where young people on bail can live in safety, and where every service they need comes to them. Legal support. Health care. Education re-engagement. Cultural connection. Transport. Family support. All coordinated through one place, so the young person does not have to navigate a fragmented system alone.

The central message: Young people in the Illawarra are being held in custody not because of what they are charged with, but because the system has no safe place to send them. YouthScape is a single, practical solution — a house where a young person on bail can live, and where ALS legal support, Medicare health services, education re-engagement, cultural connection, transport coordination, and family support all come together under one roof. The evidence from other regions shows this model works. The Illawarra needs it now.

The young people this affects

The children who end up in this situation are not hardened offenders. They are young people — sometimes as young as 10 — whose home lives have broken down. Parents struggling with drugs and alcohol. Mental health problems. Violence in the home. Chaotic environments where no adult is providing structure, safety or supervision. These young people stop attending school. They start roaming the streets at night. They get drawn into stealing, often as a survival mechanism or because there is simply nothing else to do. By the time they are caught, the damage has already been done — not by the offence, but by the circumstances that led to it.

When these young people come before Wollongong or Port Kembla Local Courts, the magistrate faces an impossible choice: send them back to the unsafe home, or remand them in custody. Neither option addresses the underlying problem. What is missing is a third option — a safe, stable place to live with wraparound support that addresses every dimension of the young person's circumstances.

What the youth detention numbers show

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander young people are detained at a rate of 354 per 100,000 young people — compared to 9 per 100,000 for non-Indigenous young people (AIHW Youth Justice in Australia, 2022-23). That is a ratio of 39 to 1, making youth detention the most disproportionate area of the entire criminal justice system. By comparison, the adult imprisonment ratio is 14.8 to 1.

Nationally, 55% of young people in detention are unsentenced — they have not been convicted of any offence. In NSW, that figure rises to 64% (NSW Bureau of Crime Statistics and Research, 2023). Nearly two in three young people in custody in NSW are waiting for their matter to be finalised, not serving a sentence. The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare reports that 38% of young people in detention had a history of homelessness or unstable housing (AIHW, 2022-23). The Australian Law Reform Commission identified this gap in its 2017 Pathways to Justice report, recommending that all jurisdictions expand culturally appropriate bail accommodation for Aboriginal young people (Recommendation 11-4). Eight years later, that recommendation remains substantially unimplemented.

In the Illawarra region specifically, approximately 13,284 Aboriginal people call this area home — 3.9% of the total population, above the NSW average of 3.4% (ABS 2021 Census). The nearest youth detention centre to Port Kembla is Reiby Youth Justice Centre in Airds, Campbelltown — 70 kilometres north. The alternative is Frank Baxter at Kariong, roughly 100 kilometres away. When a young person is remanded there, family visits become difficult or impossible, cultural connection is broken, and the relationships that are proven to reduce reoffending are severed. In 2025, the Illawarra Mercury reported that local magistrates were raising the specific problem of bail refusals due to unsafe housing, with Aboriginal community organisations calling for dedicated bail accommodation in the region.

The existing service landscape in the Illawarra: what each organisation does, and the gap none of them fill

The Illawarra has a network of Aboriginal community-controlled organisations providing vital services. Each serves a specific need. None currently provides youth bail accommodation with coordinated wraparound support. Understanding this landscape makes clear why YouthScape fills a gap that no existing organisation covers.

Illawarra Aboriginal Corporation (IAC) — 4 Wentworth Street, Port Kembla. IAC operates housing and homelessness programs, a permanency support program (child protection and out-of-home care through Myimbarr), a cultural centre, aged care, a preschool (Noogaleek & Winnanggay), and employment services (Warrigal). IAC's housing program supports Aboriginal people with access to stable housing and those at risk of homelessness, but it does not provide youth-specific crisis accommodation for young people on bail, nor does it coordinate the wraparound legal, health and education services that a young person on bail requires.

Gawura Aboriginal Corporation — 211 Lake Entrance Road, Shellharbour. Gawura runs the Brothers Against Domestic Violence (BADV) program, the ChangeV Men's Behaviour Change Program, and is a registered sponsor for Work and Development Orders and Community Service Orders through Community Corrections. Gawura provides important services for adults in the justice system, but does not operate any youth programs, accommodation, or bail support.

Waminda (South Coast Women's Health and Wellbeing Aboriginal Corporation) — based in Nowra, serving the Shoalhaven region. Waminda provides health services, maternity care, client support including family preservation, and cultural programs. They serve women and families but are primarily Shoalhaven-focused and do not provide youth bail accommodation in the Illawarra.

Aboriginal Legal Service (NSW/ACT) Wollongong — Level 1, 116 Crown Street, Wollongong. ALS runs the Youth Bail Advocacy and Support Program (YBASP), providing bail advocacy and accommodation referrals. ALS is a critical piece of the puzzle, but YBASP provides legal advocacy and referrals — it does not provide the accommodation itself, nor does it coordinate health, education, cultural and transport services.

Youth Justice NSW — 85 Market Street, Wollongong. Youth Justice NSW runs the Youth on Bail program: 24-hour supported accommodation for young people aged 10 to 17 who cannot safely return home, delivered by partner organisations including Wesley Mission and available in the Illawarra region. A 2019 NSW Audit Office evaluation found that Youth on Bail reduced remand by approximately 30% for participants. However, Youth on Bail is a program within the justice system, not a community-led model. It does not include the coordinated wraparound services — health, education, culture, family support, long-term outcome tracking — that a community-led model like YouthScape would provide.

Each of these organisations provides a vital service. None of them provides what YouthScape proposes: a single physical house where a young person on bail can live, and where every service they need — legal, health, education, cultural, transport, family support — comes to them, coordinated through a single community-led organisation.

What works: evidence from other regions

YouthScape is not a theoretical model. The same principles have been proven to work in other regions of Australia and internationally.

The Maranguka Justice Reinvestment Project in Bourke — operating under the same Local Decision Making framework as IRAAC — achieved a 14% to 41% reduction in adult bail breaches, a 42% drop in adult custody days, a 23% reduction in police-recorded domestic violence, and $3.1 million in regional and justice benefit in a single year (KPMG Independent Evaluation, 2018). Maranguka's model is built on a single coordinating backbone organisation that connects existing services rather than duplicating them — the same principle YouthScape applies.

The Youth Koori Court in Victoria shows that Aboriginal young people who go through culturally safe, community-connected justice processes have 32% lower reoffending over 24 months compared to mainstream courts. The key ingredients are cultural safety, community involvement, and coordinated support — all of which YouthScape is designed to provide.

The Youth on Bail program in NSW has demonstrated that providing supported accommodation as an alternative to remand works, with a ~30% reduction in remand for participants (NSW Audit Office, 2019). Youth on Bail provides the accommodation piece. YouthScape adds the wraparound services and community-led coordination that maximise the impact.

The ALS Youth Bail Advocacy and Support Program is cited by the ALRC as a protective factor for Aboriginal young people in bail proceedings. When a young person has ALS representation at the bail stage, they are significantly more likely to be granted bail. At YouthScape, ALS support would be coordinated as one of the services available under the same roof.

Canada's Gladue Courts require bail authorities to consider systemic factors through dedicated caseworker reports, resulting in higher bail grant rates and earlier resolution for Indigenous defendants. The Gladue model shows that when the system is required to see the whole person — their background, their circumstances, their community context — better outcomes follow. YouthScape is designed to give the Illawarra courts exactly that: a complete picture of every young person, their needs, and the support they will receive.

Internationally, New Zealand's Rangatahi Courts hold youth court proceedings on marae (communal gathering places), embedding cultural connection into the justice process and improving outcomes for Māori young people. The principle is the same: when you bring justice to the community rather than sending the young person away from it, reoffending drops.

The common thread across every proven model is clear: keeping young people connected to their community, their family and their culture, in a safe and stable environment, is the single most effective way to reduce reoffending. YouthScape is designed to do exactly this, in one house, in the Illawarra.

What YouthScape provides

YouthScape is a physical house in the Illawarra region. When a young person comes before Wollongong or Port Kembla Local Courts and cannot safely return home, the court can order them to YouthScape as a bail condition. At YouthScape, every service the young person needs comes to them, rather than the young person having to navigate a fragmented system alone.

Safe accommodation: A stable, supervised home environment with trained staff on site, providing the safety and structure that is missing from the young person's home. This alone changes the court's calculation — where previously there was no safe option, now there is YouthScape.

Legal support: ALS Wollongong caseworkers provide bail advocacy, court representation through virtual bail hearings, and ongoing legal support. Young people at YouthScape have access to laptops and technology for virtual bail court appearances, and transport to physical court hearings when needed.

Health and wellbeing: Medicare-accessible health practitioners visit YouthScape to provide physical health check-ups and mental health support. Many of these young people have never had consistent access to health care. YouthScape brings the doctor to them.

Education re-engagement: YouthScape supports young people to return to school or access alternative education pathways. For young people who have disengaged from school completely, this is often the first step back into a structured, positive routine.

Cultural connection: Connection to culture is not an optional extra at YouthScape. It is a core part of the program — taking young people back to Country, connecting them with Elders, and rebuilding the cultural identity that is essential to long-term wellbeing.

Family support: When a young person is placed in YouthScape, the support extends to their family. Parents and carers are offered drug and alcohol support, mental health services, and connection to the services they need. The goal is not just to support the young person in isolation, but to strengthen the family so that reunification is possible when it is safe.

Transport coordination: The single biggest practical barrier to bail compliance is transport. YouthScape provides transport to and from court, to ALS appointments, to police reporting, to school, and to cultural activities. This alone eliminates the most common reason bail conditions are breached.

The moment a child is placed on bail, the phone call goes out. IRAAC's coordinated referral system ensures that within hours of a young person being bailed to YouthScape, every service they need is contacted and scheduled. There is no gap between the bail hearing and the first support arriving.

YouthScape as a research engine: proving what works

One of the most important functions YouthScape serves is evidence gathering. Every young person who comes through YouthScape is interviewed and surveyed consistently and repeatedly over a number of years, using IRAAC's established survey and feedback processes — the same process that identified bail as an emerging community issue in the first place.

This gives IRAAC something that virtually no other youth bail program in Australia has: longitudinal data showing the outcomes of every young person who passes through the program. How many returned to school. How many avoided further contact with the justice system. How many re-engaged with family and culture. How their health improved. This data is not just for advocacy — it is for continuous improvement, for reporting back to government on what works, and for proving that the investment in YouthScape delivers measurable, long-term results.

Why IRAAC is best placed to deliver YouthScape

IRAAC is not a legal service, a health provider, or an education provider. What IRAAC is, and what makes it uniquely positioned to deliver YouthScape, is a community-led coordination organisation operating under the Local Decision Making framework — the same framework that delivered the Maranguka results in Bourke.

Existing trust and reach: IRAAC already operates four contact channels — book a call, complete the survey, visit an office, request a home visit — that community members use to raise issues including bail-related barriers. The community already knows IRAAC. The referral infrastructure already exists.

Established listening loop: IRAAC's core model — You share, We listen, We recommend to government, We report back — ensures that community feedback on what is and is not working is captured, reported to government, and used to improve the service. No other organisation in the Illawarra has this feedback infrastructure.

Proven governance: IRAAC operates with a standard Board agenda and minutes framework, an annual independent Board evaluation, role-specific training, and regular reporting to Aboriginal Affairs NSW, ORIC and the ACNC. Government can fund YouthScape through IRAAC with confidence that the money will be transparently accounted for.

Coordination, not duplication: IRAAC does not need to replicate what ALS, IAC, Gawura, Waminda or Youth Justice NSW already do. YouthScape connects them. ALS provides the legal advocacy. Medicare-accessible providers deliver the health care. Schools provide the education pathways. IAC provides cultural services. Youth Justice NSW provides the Youth on Bail framework. What is missing is the community-led coordination that makes sure every young person gets all of these services, at the same place, at the same time. IRAAC is the organisation that can provide that coordination.

Recommendations

Based on the evidence in this report, IRAAC makes the following recommendations:

  1. Fund YouthScape as a physical bail accommodation house in the Illawarra — a single property where young people on bail can live safely, with trained staff on site and a coordinated wraparound service model
  2. Fund IRAAC to coordinate the YouthScape service model — managing referral pathways from Wollongong and Port Kembla Local Courts, coordinating ALS legal support, health services, education re-engagement, cultural connection, transport, and family support under one roof
  3. Establish a formal partnership agreement between IRAAC, ALS Wollongong, Youth Justice NSW, and Youth on Bail providers to ensure every Aboriginal young person in the Illawarra has a guaranteed pathway from bail hearing to safe accommodation with wraparound support
  4. Fund transport coordination within the Illawarra so that young people on bail can get to ALS appointments, court appearances, school, and police reporting without breaching conditions
  5. Fund the longitudinal research and surveying program that tracks every young person through YouthScape over multiple years, building the evidence base for what works in reducing youth remand and reoffending

How IRAAC will deliver

IRAAC commits to the following specific actions:

  • Apply for funding to establish YouthScape as described in this report, working with ALS Wollongong, Youth Justice NSW, Illawarra Aboriginal Corporation, Gawura Aboriginal Corporation and other service partners to design a coordinated service model
  • Build the referral infrastructure connecting Wollongong and Port Kembla Local Courts, ALS Wollongong, and Youth on Bail providers to YouthScape, ensuring warm handovers with named contacts
  • Report to government on what is working and what needs to change, using community feedback and longitudinal data as the primary evidence base
  • Report back to community on what changed as a result of their input — the listening-to-advocacy loop is not complete until community members know their voice led to action
  • Continue surveying and interviewing every young person who passes through YouthScape, building a longitudinal dataset that does not exist anywhere else in Australia's youth bail system
  • Advocate for the broader systemic reforms needed while delivering practical help in the communities IRAAC already serves

A note on how this evidence was gathered

This report is built on community feedback gathered through IRAAC's Have Your Say survey, direct conversations with community members, and analysis of national data from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, the Australian Bureau of Statistics, the NSW Bureau of Crime Statistics and Research, the Australian Law Reform Commission, the Australian Institute of Criminology, and published evaluations of justice programs. No identifying participant information is included. IRAAC will continue listening to community, protect personal information, and test its interpretation with Aboriginal community representatives before presenting broader conclusions.

Bail Conditions: An Emerging Community Issue

Community feedback has raised bail conditions as an issue connected with Local Decision Making. The same feedback also identified transport support and uncertainty about the best next step. Taken together, these points suggest that bail can involve practical access and service-navigation challenges as well as legal requirements.

This enhanced report shares an early community signal for Aboriginal community members, government organisations, frontline workers and service partners, supported by national data, legal analysis, and evidence from proven programs. It does not identify anyone, provide legal advice or claim to describe every person's experience. Its purpose is to guide further listening and identify practical opportunities for culturally safe support.

The central message: Understanding bail conditions is not enough on its own. People also need clear information, reliable transport, stable housing, a trusted person to speak with, and a coordinated pathway to qualified legal and practical support. Australia's major inquiries have made the same recommendations for 34 years with negligible implementation. Community-led action is no longer optional — it is essential.

The national picture: what the data shows

According to the Australian Bureau of Statistics Prisoners in Australia 2024, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander adults are incarcerated at a rate of 2,617 per 100,000 — compared to 177 per 100,000 for non-Indigenous adults. That is a ratio of 14.8 to 1. For remand specifically, the ratio is even higher: Indigenous people are 16 times more likely to be held on remand.

Nationally, 41% of all Indigenous prisoners are unsentenced — they have not been convicted of any offence. They are waiting for their matter to be finalised, often because they cannot meet bail conditions designed for circumstances that bear no resemblance to their own.

The Australian Institute of Criminology's Bail and Remand in Australia report (2024) found that 66% of Indigenous remandees did not have stable housing at the time of their arrest, compared to 35% of non-Indigenous remandees. 42% reported that lack of transport prevented them from complying with bail conditions — nearly double the rate for non-Indigenous people.

These are not questions about guilt or innocence. They are questions about whether the bail system accommodates the realities of Aboriginal life in Australia. On the current evidence, it does not.

What the community is telling us

IRAAC's July 2026 Monthly Survey captured questions directly from Aboriginal community members about the practical barriers they face. These questions are directly relevant to bail because the same barriers — transport, housing, needing a trusted contact — are what prevent people from complying with bail conditions.

Community members asked: Can an IRAAC worker visit my home or community if getting to an office is difficult? What support is available for families and for people dealing with several needs at once? Can practical barriers, including transport and access, be considered when support is arranged?

These questions tell us that the same problems driving the national remand crisis are the problems people face every day just trying to access support. A person who cannot get to an IRAAC office for a conversation also cannot get to a police station to report for bail, or to a court for a hearing.

What the law requires

The High Court of Australia has established clear principles. In Bugmy v The Queen [2013] HCA 37, the Court unanimously held that the effects of childhood deprivation do not diminish with time or repeat offending, and that "full weight" must be given to this consideration in every decision affecting an Aboriginal person in the criminal justice system — including bail. In Fernando (1992) 76 A Crim R 58, the NSW Supreme Court set out eight principles requiring courts to consider community circumstances and systemic disadvantage.

Section 3A of the Bail Act 2013 (NSW) requires bail authorities to consider a person's Aboriginal cultural background, cultural obligations, and any issues arising from them in every bail decision. However, the ALRC found this provision is inconsistently applied — often treated as a box-ticking exercise. Only NSW and Victoria have this provision. The ALRC recommended in 2017 that all jurisdictions adopt it. Six of eight have not.

The UN Special Rapporteur on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples specifically identified NSW's "show cause" bail provisions as discriminatory during his 2023 visit to Australia. Australia's obligations under UNDRIP (Articles 2, 7, 10) and the Tokyo Rules (requiring pre-trial detention as a "means of last resort") reinforce the case for reform.

What has already been said — and not done

The same recommendations about bail and Aboriginal over-incarceration have been made by every major inquiry for 34 years:

  • Royal Commission into Aboriginal Deaths in Custody (1991): 339 recommendations including bail-specific ones. The Indigenous imprisonment rate has doubled since. 474 additional deaths in custody.
  • ALRC Pathways to Justice (2017): 35 recommendations, five bail-specific. No jurisdiction has fully implemented them.
  • NSW Select Committee on High Aboriginal Incarceration (2022): Recommended abolition of "show cause" for Aboriginal young people. The NSW Government rejected key recommendations.
  • UN Special Rapporteur (2023): Identified "show cause" as discriminatory. No legislative response.
  • Closing the Gap (2020): Targets 10 and 11 (reducing incarceration and detention) are both not on track. The incarceration rate has increased since the targets were set.

The problems are well understood, the solutions are well documented, and the implementation has been negligible. This is why community-led action is the only path that has not been tried.

What works: the ALS Nowra bail advocacy model

Among all proven bail support models, the Aboriginal Legal Service (NSW/ACT) Bail Advocacy Program is the most immediately scalable solution. Unlike justice reinvestment projects (which require years of multi-agency coordination) or specialised court models (which need legislative reform), ALS operates entirely within the existing legal framework. It requires no new legislation, no new courtrooms, and no structural changes to the judiciary. It simply needs funding.

The ALS operates an office at 89 Plunkett Street, Nowra, with a dedicated Family Violence Prevention Unit at Unit 2, 64 North Street. ALS Nowra already provides criminal law representation, bail advocacy, and culturally safe legal support to Aboriginal people across the South Coast region. The office is staffed by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander caseworkers who understand the community, the kinship structures, and the practical barriers people face when navigating the bail system.

But ALS Nowra is operating under severe, ongoing funding constraints. Peak bodies report that funding has fallen in real terms while regional case demands have doubled. High staff burnout and localised “service freezes” occur when caseloads become unsustainable. Expanding ALS Nowra is the single most effective and most achievable step the NSW Government can take to reduce Aboriginal remand rates in the region.

The virtual bail crisis

The NSW Government has centralised urgent bail hearings through virtual bail courts — where a person refused bail by regional police appears via video link rather than in a physical courtroom. In principle, this should improve access. In practice, it has created a new set of problems.

Virtual bail hearings were rolled out without dedicated funding for ALS to staff them. ALS Nowra lawyers are expected to appear in virtual hearings on top of their existing caseloads, with no additional resources for the video-link technology, preparation time, or the extended hours these hearings require. The result is that Aboriginal people in the Nowra region go into bail hearings without proper representation, without a caseworker who has had time to prepare a complete picture of their circumstances, and without the advocacy that could make the difference between remand and release on culturally appropriate conditions.

The long-term solution is not to scrap virtual bail courts — they reduce the need for people to travel long distances to court, which is itself a barrier. The solution is to fund ALS Nowra properly to staff them: dedicated virtual bail lawyers, video-link infrastructure at the ALS office, and compensation for after-hours work. This is a straightforward resourcing problem, not a legislative one, and it can be solved immediately with targeted funding.

How IRAAC can oversee and manage ALS Nowra funding

IRAAC is not a legal service and does not provide legal advice. What IRAAC can do — and what the evidence in this report demonstrates IRAAC is uniquely positioned to do — is manage the funding, coordination, and accountability framework that allows ALS Nowra to operate at full capacity.

Funding oversight: IRAAC's governance structure — standard Board agenda and minutes framework, annual independent Board evaluation, role-specific training, and regular reporting to Aboriginal Affairs NSW, ORIC, and the ACNC — means any funding directed through IRAAC can be properly acquitted and reported against. Government can fund IRAAC with confidence that the money will be spent for its intended purpose and transparently accounted for.

Coordinated referral pathways: IRAAC already operates the four direct contact channels — book a call, complete the survey, visit an office, request a home visit — that community members use to raise issues including bail-related barriers. IRAAC can build structured referral pathways from these channels directly to ALS Nowra, ensuring that every person who needs bail advocacy reaches a qualified caseworker without falling through gaps.

Transport and practical support: The single biggest barrier to bail compliance in the Nowra region is transport. IRAAC, with appropriate funding, can provide transport coordination — getting people from their communities to the ALS office, to court, and to police reporting stations — alongside ALS's legal advocacy. This directly addresses the 42% of Indigenous remandees who report lack of transport as a barrier to bail compliance.

Closing the listening loop: IRAAC's core model — You share, We listen, We recommend to government, We report back — ensures that community feedback on what is and is not working with ALS Nowra services is captured, reported to government, and used to improve the service. This is the accountability mechanism that government-run programs consistently lack.

What this means for ALS Nowra, concretely

With adequate funding overseen and coordinated by IRAAC, ALS Nowra could:

  • Hire additional solicitors and caseworkers to eliminate service freezes and ensure every Aboriginal person in the Nowra region has access to bail advocacy when they need it
  • Fund dedicated virtual bail court staff so that virtual hearings are properly resourced and defendants are not appearing without representation
  • Expand the Family Violence Prevention Unit to include non-legal caseworkers who can bridge the gap between court representation and immediate safety planning, reducing family out-of-home care interventions
  • Purchase and install video-link technology at the Plunkett Street office for virtual bail hearings, so defendants can speak with their ALS lawyer privately before appearing before a magistrate on screen
  • Partner with IRAAC for transport coordination so that getting to the ALS office, to court, and to police reporting is no longer a barrier to bail compliance
  • Provide outreach services to Aboriginal communities across the South Coast, not just in Nowra itself — meeting people where they are rather than expecting them to travel

Why this model works: evidence from what already exists

The evidence base for this approach is strong. The ALS bail advocacy program is consistently cited by the ALRC Pathways to Justice report as a protective factor — meaning that Aboriginal people who have ALS representation at the bail stage are significantly more likely to be granted bail than those who do not. The Maranguka Justice Reinvestment Project in Bourke — a community-led program operating under the same LDM framework as IRAAC — achieved a 14% to 41% reduction in adult bail breaches, a 42% drop in adult custody days, a 23% reduction in police-recorded domestic violence, and a 31% rise in Year 12 student retention, delivering $3.1 million in regional and justice benefit in a single year (KPMG Independent Evaluation, 2018).

Victoria's Koori Courts show 32% lower reoffending over 24 months compared to mainstream courts. Canada's Gladue Courts — requiring bail authorities to consider systemic factors through dedicated caseworker reports — provide an international template that Australia should adopt, but which requires legislative change. The ALS + IRAAC model does not need legislative change. It can be funded and operational within a budget cycle.

These are not theoretical projections. These are measured outcomes from programs operating under the same framework IRAAC operates within.

Recommendations to government

Based on community feedback, national data, legal principles, and proven models, IRAAC makes the following recommendations. The first five are the immediate priority for the Nowra region. Recommendations six to ten are broader systemic reforms that IRAAC will continue to advocate for.

Immediate priorities for the Nowra region:

  1. Fund ALS Nowra for additional solicitors and caseworkers to eliminate service freezes and ensure every Aboriginal person in the region has access to bail advocacy. Direct funding through IRAAC for oversight, acquittal, and coordination.
  2. Fund dedicated virtual bail court resources for ALS Nowra — including video-link infrastructure at the Plunkett Street office, dedicated virtual bail lawyers, and compensation for after-hours hearings. This is the single most urgent gap in the current system.
  3. Establish IRAAC as the transport coordination partner for ALS Nowra — providing funded transport to court, police reporting, and ALS appointments. Transport is the #1 practical barrier for 42% of Indigenous remandees.
  4. Expand the ALS Nowra Family Violence Prevention Unit to include non-legal caseworkers for early intervention and safety planning, reducing the number of Aboriginal women and children entering the out-of-home care system through bail-related pathways.
  5. Fund IRAAC to build structured referral pathways from its four contact channels (call, survey, drop-in, home visit) directly to ALS Nowra, ensuring every community member who needs bail advocacy is connected to a qualified caseworker.

Broader systemic reforms IRAAC will continue to advocate for:

  1. Adopt ALRC Recommendation 5-1 in all jurisdictions — requiring bail authorities to consider Aboriginal cultural background. Six of eight jurisdictions lack this.
  2. Reform "show cause" provisions that disproportionately affect Aboriginal defendants, as identified by the UN Special Rapporteur and the NSW 2022 parliamentary inquiry.
  3. Invest in culturally safe bail accommodation — 66% of Indigenous remandees lack stable housing.
  4. Mandate data collection on bail outcomes by Indigenous status, gender, age, and location (ALRC Recommendation 5-2).
  5. Adopt justice reinvestment as the overarching framework for reducing remand, scaling the Maranguka model across NSW.

Building the evidence responsibly

This is an emerging signal rather than a population estimate. Community feedback is indicative of themes rather than a statistically representative sample. The national data is drawn from the most authoritative sources available (ABS, AIHW, AIC). Monthly Survey ReportJuly 2026

IRAAC Survey Monthly Report

This month, community members used IRAAC's survey to ask about culture, young people, family and community support, programs, practical help and the best way to speak with someone. These questions are important because they show that people are not only looking for information. They are looking for a clear, respectful way to connect with a person who can listen, explain what is available and help work out the next step.

This report brings those questions together for Aboriginal community members, government organisations and the workers who meet people every day. It does not identify anyone or repeat personal circumstances. Its purpose is to share the themes that were raised and turn them into practical guidance for stronger, more culturally responsive service.

The central message: people want more than a list of services. They want welcoming, face-to-face and flexible pathways into support, with culture, family and community understood as part of the whole picture.

The sorts of questions community members asked

  • Where can I sit down and speak with someone face to face?
  • Can an IRAAC worker visit my home or community if getting to an office is difficult?
  • What support is available for families and for people dealing with several needs at once?
  • How can young people take part in activities, stay connected to culture and feel supported?
  • What programs, events and community activities are available, and how do people join?
  • Can practical barriers, including transport and access, be considered when support is arranged?
  • Who will contact me, what happens next, and how will I know my question has reached the right person?

What the questions tell us

Connection comes before service navigation. People may know what is happening in their life without knowing the name of the program or government area that fits. A good first response should therefore begin with listening, not asking people to diagnose the system for themselves.

Face-to-face contact still matters. Interest in local offices, home or community visits and direct follow-up shows the value of human contact. Digital forms can open the door, but they should lead to a real conversation rather than become another place where a person has to repeat their story.

Culture, young people and family are connected. Questions about cultural connection and activities for young people sit alongside questions about family and practical support. This suggests that cultural participation should not be treated as an optional extra. It can be part of belonging, confidence, prevention and longer-term wellbeing.

Needs do not arrive one at a time. Some questions point to interconnected practical, family and service-access needs. Sending a person to several separate services without coordination can create more work and frustration. A warm handover, a named contact and shared responsibility are more useful than a list of phone numbers.

Access is part of the service. Transport, location, timing and the choice of where to meet can determine whether a person is able to participate at all. Flexible ways to engage should be planned from the beginning, not added only after someone has struggled.

What this means for each audience

For Aboriginal community members

You should not need to know the right program name before asking for help. You can start with what matters to you and choose how you want to talk: by phone, at an office, through the survey, or by asking about a home or community visit. Asking about culture, family, young people, activities or practical support is a valid place to begin.

For government organisations

The questions reinforce the need to fund and design services around relationships, cultural safety and coordinated follow-up. Success should include whether people reach the right worker, understand the next step and remain connected — not only whether a referral was issued or a form was completed.

For frontline workers

Begin with the person's priorities. Explain choices in plain language, check how and where they would prefer to meet, ask about practical barriers and make a warm handover where another service is needed. Before ending the conversation, confirm who owns the next action and when the person can expect to hear back.

For community and service partners

Keep information current, share clear entry points and work together when needs cross organisational boundaries. Community-led programs and mainstream services are strongest when each understands its role, respects consent and avoids making people retell the same story unnecessarily.

Practical priorities for the month ahead

  • Keep the four direct contact choices visible: book a call, complete the survey, visit an office or request a home visit.
  • Respond in plain language and tell each person what will happen next, who is responsible and when follow-up is expected.
  • Make it easier to ask for help without first choosing a program or service category.
  • Build transport, location and other access needs into the first conversation.
  • Strengthen pathways for cultural connection, young people's participation, family support and community activities.
  • Use warm handovers and coordinated follow-up when a person's needs involve more than one organisation.
  • Continue protecting privacy while reporting back to community on the themes being heard and the actions being taken.

Continuing the conversation

Monthly reporting is one way IRAAC can show that community questions are being heard. The next step is to keep the conversation open, make follow-up easier and return to community with clear information about what changed. If something is important to you, you can raise it in the way that feels most comfortable.

GovernanceJuly 2026

Why Governance Is the Foundation of Strong Community Programs

It's easy to think of governance as paperwork — the meetings, the minutes, the reports that sit behind the programs people actually see and feel the benefit of. But good governance is what makes those programs possible in the first place. A Board that meets regularly, keeps clear records of its decisions, and reports honestly on what's working and what isn't is a Board that funders, partners and community can trust with more responsibility over time.

For IRAAC, that means treating governance as an everyday discipline rather than something to scramble for when a report is due. A standard agenda and minutes framework, an annual independent Board evaluation, and role-specific training for Board Members and staff all sound unglamorous — but they're exactly what lets us say, with confidence, that IRAAC is well run. That confidence is what frees us up to focus on the programs themselves: MCC, YouthScape, The Crew and DARC.

Under Local Decision Making, this matters even more. The more clearly an organisation like IRAAC can demonstrate strong governance, the stronger the case for more decisions being made locally rather than centrally. Good governance isn't the opposite of community control — it's what community control is built on.

MCCJune 2026

What We're Learning Through MCC's Peer-to-Peer Model

MCC — Mob and Country Connections — started from a simple observation: IRAAC had spent real time and effort building governance, administration and reporting systems for itself, and other Aboriginal Community Organisations were often working through the exact same challenges from scratch. Rather than keep that work inside IRAAC, MCC shares it — the templates, the training, the lessons learned — with organisations that ask for it.

The model only works because support is offered at the invitation of the organisation receiving it. Nobody likes being told how to run their organisation by an outsider, and Aboriginal Community Organisations have particular reason to be wary of top-down models imposed from elsewhere. MCC is peer-to-peer by design: IRAAC has walked the path already, and shares what it learned rather than prescribing a fixed solution.

It's early days, and MCC is as much a live experiment as it is a finished program — a chance for IRAAC to test whether Aboriginal-led, relationship-based capability sharing genuinely moves the needle for other organisations, and to keep refining the approach based on what those organisations tell us actually helps.

Local Decision MakingMay 2026

Local Decision Making: Turning Policy Into Practice

Local Decision Making sounds straightforward on paper: give Aboriginal communities a stronger say in the decisions that affect them. In practice, it depends entirely on the structures underneath it actually working — the regional Alliances that identify priorities, the community Assemblies that keep those priorities grounded in what people have actually said, and organisations like IRAAC that turn those priorities into programs on the ground.

For IRAAC, that means staying closely connected to its regional Alliance and Assembly rather than assuming it already knows what community needs. It also means being transparent about where IRAAC sits in the wider system — working alongside Aboriginal Affairs NSW as a government partner, not being run by it, and reporting to ORIC and the ACNC as any responsible charity and corporation should.

The test of whether Local Decision Making is working isn't the policy documents — it's whether the programs communities actually receive reflect what those communities said they wanted. That's the standard IRAAC is trying to hold itself to, and it's one we'll keep coming back to in future updates.
